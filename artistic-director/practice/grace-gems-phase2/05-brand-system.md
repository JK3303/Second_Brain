## 2. Verbal Identity

### Brand Voice

Grace Gems speaks in the second person. The customer is the subject of every sentence that matters. The stones, the craft, the maker — these serve the customer's story, not the other way around.

### Voice Principles

**Warm, not precious.** Write like you're talking to someone who's excited about a meaningful purchase, not someone you're trying to impress. "We'll find the right stone for what you have in mind" — not "Discover our exquisite collection of curated gemstones."

**Specific, not superlative.** Name the stone, the origin, the cut, the metal weight. Specificity builds trust; adjectives don't. "A 2.1-carat Zambian emerald in 18k rose gold" does more work than "a stunning emerald ring."

**Occasion-aware, not occasion-dependent.** Many pieces mark a moment — but some are just for the wearer. Never assume why someone is buying. "Whether it's for someone you love or just for you" keeps the door open.

**Collaborative, not transactional.** The custom process is a conversation, not an order form. "Tell us what you're imagining" — not "Submit your custom order request."

**Anonymous maker.** The craftsman behind the work is not named or individually featured. The voice is "we" — the Grace Gems team. Skill and care come through in the work itself, not a personal brand.

These are starting points for creative development. None are approved for use.

## 3. Visual Identity Framework

### Color Palette Direction

The palette takes its cues from the stones themselves — but softened and warmed for the brand environment. The stones provide the drama; the brand environment provides the warmth.

**Primary brand tones:**
- Warm white / cream (background — not clinical white)
- Soft gold (accent — referencing 18k gold, not metallic glare)
- Charcoal (text — warm-toned, not pure black)

**Gemstone accent palette** (used sparingly, drawn from actual product categories):
- Deep emerald green
- Pigeon-blood red
- Teal tourmaline
- Ocean sapphire blue
- Opal fire spectrum (iridescent — used in photography, not UI)

**Avoid:**
- Pure white backgrounds (too clinical, too Etsy-default)
- Millennial pink / blush as a primary (too crowded, too generic in jewelry)
- Black-and-gold luxury coding (wrong price-point signal)

### Typography Direction

- **Headlines:** A humanist serif — warm, readable at large sizes, with personality but not ornamentation. Think: something with the confidence of a Garamond but the warmth of a Charter or Freight Text.
- **Body:** A clean, well-spaced sans-serif — readable, modern, friendly. Not geometric (too cold) or grotesque (too neutral). Something like a Söhne or an Untitled Sans.
- **Accent/detail:** The serif at small sizes for captions, metadata, and stone specifications.

### Photography Direction

This is the most critical brand asset. The visual language must show jewelry in life, not in a void.

**Primary photography mode: On-person, in-context.**
- Jewelry shown being worn — hands, necks, ears — in warm, natural light
- Skin tones and textures are visible; the jewelry exists in relationship to a body
- Backgrounds suggest real environments (a table, a window, fabric) without being literal lifestyle photography
- The wearer's face is usually not visible — the focus is the piece and the gesture

**Secondary photography mode: Stone portrait.**
- Close macro of the stone itself — its color, its inclusions, its light behavior
- Dark or neutral backgrounds that let the stone's natural properties lead
- These are the "material truth" images — they prove the quality the brand voice claims

**Avoid:**
- Floating-on-white product shots as the primary mode (this is what Etsy already does)
- Over-styled lifestyle scenes (champagne glasses, rose petals, candlelit dinners)
- Stock photography of any kind
- Models who look like jewelry ads — the imagery should feel personal, not aspirational

### Logo Direction

**Approach: Wordmark-first.**

Grace Gems doesn't need a symbol or icon at this stage. The name is short, memorable, and distinctive enough to carry itself as a wordmark in the chosen serif typeface.

A secondary mark (a monogram "GG" or a minimal stone-derived element) could develop over time from actual creative cycles — but should not be forced at the territory stage.

**Principles for the wordmark:**
- Set in the brand serif at a weight that feels confident but not heavy
- Letter-spacing that gives each letter room to breathe
- Works at small sizes (favicon, jewelry box stamp, social avatar)
- No tagline locked to the logo — the tagline should live independently

**Missing:** Any existing logo or visual mark. If Robert has one, it should be assessed before creating anything new.

## 4. Brand Architecture

### How the pieces fit together

```
Grace Gems (master brand)
├── Collections (organized by stone family)
│   ├── Emerald
│   ├── Ruby
│   ├── Opal
│   ├── Tourmaline
│   ├── Sapphire
│   └── ... (remaining stone families)
├── Made for You (the personalized experience)
├── Stories (customer narratives — the brand's primary content)
└── The Workshop (process, sourcing, craft — supporting content)
```

This architecture puts Collections and Custom at the same level — neither is a secondary offering. Stories is a first-class section, not a blog afterthought. The Workshop (maker content) supports the brand but doesn't lead it — honoring Territory C's customer-centric premise while preserving Territory A's maker warmth as a supporting element.
