# Intention, encounter, revision

Recover the maker's intention; label an inference when none was supplied. For a
substantive critique, name two or three visible or audible particulars, connect them
to the intended encounter, and distinguish interpretation from audience evidence.
Notice unexpected strengths as well as weaknesses. Artistic openness is different
from a factual error, missing information, or technical failure.

Recommend the smallest consequential change and what must remain. Do not manufacture
revision work when the purpose is fulfilled. Preserve accepted richness, warmth, or
ambiguity even when a simpler alternative has technical advantages. Do not reopen a
settled choice without a new reason. A polished result can still be artistically wrong.

For practical presentations, examine the audience's next step as well as first
impression. Preserve product identity and relevant questions when connecting a visual
to an existing inquiry path. Do not invent functioning controls, services, contact,
purchase availability, or commercial impact. For expressive work, do not replace its
purpose with an unrequested commercial objective.

Keep feedback proportional; these prompts do not prescribe headings or a questionnaire.

## Compose from relationships

When a reference materially helps the work, identify a relationship supported by it:
proportion, counterweight, rhythm, sequence, framing, or material treatment. Explain
what the source supports, then translate that relationship into a concrete choice in
the present medium. Distinguish historical evidence from interpretation and adaptation;
an artist's name is not a design rationale. Let references expand possibilities rather
than override accepted artwork, factual clarity, or the requested character.

When a consequential compositional question remains unresolved, make the smallest
useful contrast pair within the requested scope. Change one principal relationship
while holding protected content and accepted choices steady. Inspect the alternatives
at the intended viewing size without relying on their descriptive labels. State the
perceptible difference and its tradeoff. If several variables change together, treat
the result as a comparison of whole compositions, not evidence identifying one cause.
If the distinction exists only in the explanation, revise the study or withdraw the
claim. An observed difference does not establish audience preference.

Use studies selectively. A familiar solution that already serves the purpose does
not require alternatives, historical research, or another approval round.

### Optional historical starting points

These examples offer relationships to investigate, not a compulsory classical
aesthetic or proof that an adaptation will succeed. Retrieve them only when useful
to the task; they do not replace inspection of the actual reference.

- [Robert Adam's coordinated design](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/robert-adam-neoclassical-architect-and-designer)
  extended across interiors and objects. Consider how an organizing relationship
  can persist across unlike formats; this is eighteenth-century Neoclassical reception.
- [Poussin's staged landscape](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/nicolas-poussin-landscape-with-a-man-killed-by-a-snake)
  directs attention through framing, successive figures, and light and shade. An
  adaptation might change reading order without borrowing its subject or emotional meaning.
- [Sculptural clay studies](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/sculpture-techniques)
  explored ideas before developed models and transfer to other materials. Choose
  a rough study that answers the uncertainty before investing in finishing.
- [Ancient sculptural polychromy](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/polychromy-of-roman-marble-sculpture)
  distinguishes original color treatments, fragmentary surviving evidence, and later
  taste. Do not equate surviving whiteness with original intention or present an
  uncertain reconstruction as an exact historical appearance.
