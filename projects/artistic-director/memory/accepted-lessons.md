# Accepted Lessons

Human-approved reusable lessons that guide future creative work. Each lesson includes the rule, the evidence from the cycle that produced it, and the conditions under which it applies.

## Lesson 1: Protect the reveal

**Rule:** When a logo or visual concept has a deliberate reveal (a secondary image in negative space, a hidden meaning, a dual read), protect that reveal at all costs. Don't add competing visual elements that split the viewer's attention or obscure the discovery moment. The surprise is the concept — diluting it dilutes everything.

**Evidence:** Rootmind Exercise 1. The leaf-brain negative space was the core concept. Adding a geometric bisecting line created visual competition and made the brain read optional rather than intentional. Removing the line restored the concept's power.

**Applies when:** Any design relies on a layered read, negative space, hidden imagery, or a "wait, I see it now" moment. The principle extends beyond logos to any creative work where the discovery is the hook.

## Lesson 2: Lead with the customer's intent, not the catalog

**Rule:** When a brand sells personalized or custom products, organize the entry experience around the customer's purpose — not the product taxonomy. Ask "what moment are you marking?" before showing product categories. Intent-based navigation turns browsing into self-reflection, which primes custom inquiries and emotional connection with the brand.

**Evidence:** Grace Gems Phase 2. The "What moment are you marking?" section with occasion tiles (Engagement, Anniversary, Milestone, Self-gift) was validated by the Human AD as the strongest element of the homepage wireframe. It resonated because it makes the customer's story the starting point rather than stone type or product category.

**Applies when:** The brand's value proposition is personal meaning, customization, or occasion-driven purchasing. Especially relevant for jewelry, bespoke goods, and any product where the "why" matters more than the "what."

## Lesson 3: Distinguish territories by the business future, not the visual treatment

**Rule:** When generating creative territories for a rebrand, separate them by the *business future* each one implies — person-led, product-led, service-led, institution-led — not by palette, typography, or photographic style. Territories that differ only in treatment are stylistic variants of one idea, and presenting them as a choice gives the decision-maker no real decision. Test each pair on four axes: centre of gravity, customer promise, proof mechanism, and the content engine the brand must keep feeding. Two axes minimum, or it is not a separate territory.

**Evidence:** One rebranding cohort. Three territories built around a maker, a material, and a method mapped onto three genuinely different company futures with different scaling limits and different key-person risk. The decision-maker was choosing a business shape, not a look. Four discarded candidates failed the same test on one axis or none.

**Applies when:** Generating or shortlisting creative territories, especially where the client's growth intent is unknown. Putting one territory on each side of an unresolved strategic fork lets the client answer it by choosing, instead of the team guessing it in advance.

**Confidence:** Single engagement. Accepted by the AD as a role principle rather than held for further evidence, because the failure mode it prevents — three finishes on one idea presented as a choice — is recognisable without needing a second case to confirm it.

## Lesson 4: Preserving uncertainty has creative cost, and the cost is acceptable

**Rule:** Refusing to invent facts will sometimes kill otherwise strong creative work. Kill it anyway. When a territory, a name, or a piece of copy requires a fact nobody has supplied — a founding story, a customer motivation, an origin claim — the options are to source it, to build without it, or to drop the idea. Fabricating it is not among them, and neither is quietly softening it into something that reads as fact.

**Evidence:** One rebranding cohort. A heritage-led territory was dropped entirely because it needed a founding narrative nobody could supply, and inventing one was prohibited. A dated establishment device was cut from another territory for the same reason. Sample copy in a third deliberately withheld specific origin attributions the territory was otherwise built to showcase, because those attributions came from second-hand summaries rather than the client.

**Applies when:** Always, and most sharply when the invented detail would be small, plausible, and improve the work. That is exactly when it is most tempting and least visible — a fabricated founding year looks like research, not invention, to everyone downstream.

**Confidence:** Single engagement. Accepted by the AD as a values commitment rather than an empirical finding — it does not require statistical support, and it would not become more true with a second case.
